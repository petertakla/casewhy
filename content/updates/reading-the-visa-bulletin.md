**Short answer:** The Visa Bulletin tells you whether a green card number is available for your category and country this month. If the date listed for your category is *later* than your priority date — or the entry says "C" for Current — a number is available. If it's earlier, you wait.

## The four terms

**Priority date.** Your place in line. For family-based cases it's the date USCIS received the I-130 petition; for most employment-based cases it's the date the labor certification was filed, or the I-140 if no certification was needed ([USCIS, visa availability and priority dates](https://www.uscis.gov/green-card/green-card-processes-and-procedures/visa-availability-priority-dates)). It never changes once set.

**Final Action Date.** The State Department's monthly cutoff: cases with a priority date *before* this date can be approved this month. This is the chart people usually mean when they say "the bulletin moved" ([State Department Visa Bulletin](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/legal/visa-law0/visa-bulletin.html)).

**Dates for Filing.** A second, usually later, cutoff. If USCIS announces for a given month that it's using this chart, applicants whose priority date is before it may *file* an adjustment application even though a number isn't available yet for approval. USCIS posts which chart applies each month ([USCIS filing charts page](https://www.uscis.gov/green-card/green-card-processes-and-procedures/visa-availability-priority-dates/adjustment-of-status-filing-charts-from-the-visa-bulletin)).

**Current ("C").** No backlog. Every qualified applicant in that category and country has a number available, regardless of priority date.

## Why the same category has five columns

The bulletin lists "All Chargeability Areas" plus separate columns for China, India, Mexico, and the Philippines. That's because per-country limits in the law mean demand from those four countries exceeds their annual share, so their cutoff dates are usually further back. Your column is determined by chargeability — normally your country of birth, not citizenship.

## Forward, back, and "retrogression"

Dates usually move forward month to month, but not always. When more people become eligible than the annual allocation can cover, the State Department can move a cutoff *backward* — retrogression. A case that was inside the cutoff last month can fall back outside it. That's not a decision on your case; it's the math of the annual cap.

## How to read it for yourself, in three steps

1. Find your preference category on the family- or employment-based chart.
2. Find your chargeability column.
3. Compare the date shown to your priority date. Earlier than yours: wait. Later than yours, or "C": a number is available this month.

Then check USCIS's filing-charts page for the same month to see whether the Final Action or Dates for Filing chart governs adjustment filings.
