# Which languages CaseWhy should translate into, and how/when

**Status: research and recommendation, not yet authorized to build.** This is v2/post-launch territory — the English product hasn't even reached paid launch yet (still gated on USCIS production API access) — but worth having a real, sourced answer ready rather than guessing when it comes up again.

## Which languages — real numbers, not intuition

Two data sources point the same direction, and it's worth using both rather than either alone: general US Limited-English-Proficiency (LEP) population size (who's actually out there) cross-checked against USCIS's own citizenship-outreach language list (who USCIS itself has decided matters most for naturalization specifically — CaseWhy's own current case-type scope).

**General LEP population, US Census ACS 2020-2024 (Table B16001) — people who speak English less than "very well":**

Spanish dwarfs everything else: **17.2 million** LEP speakers — nearly 10x the next-largest language. After that: Chinese (1.8M), Vietnamese (902K), Korean (556K), Tagalog/Filipino (541K), Arabic (486K), Russian (423K), Haitian Creole (374K), Portuguese (369K), French (265K), Hindi (157K), German (128K).

**USCIS's own priority list for citizenship (N-400) outreach specifically** (its Language Access Plan's 13-language toolkit list): Spanish, Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean, Tagalog, Haitian Creole, Arabic, Farsi, Burmese, Kurdish, Somali, plus English. Worth noting this list includes some languages (Burmese, Kurdish, Somali, Farsi) that aren't in the general-population top 10 — these are refugee/asylee-heavy communities, whose immigration pathway means a much higher share of that population is actively naturalizing at any given time, even though the raw US population count is smaller. That's a real, non-obvious reason USCIS itself treats them as priority languages for exactly the case type (N-400) CaseWhy already covers.

**One current-events wrinkle worth knowing:** a March 2025 executive order revoked the 25-year-old requirement (Executive Order 13166) that federal agencies provide LEP language access at all — it's now discretionary, agency by agency, not mandatory. DHS/USCIS appears to be continuing its multilingual resources in practice (per Migration Policy Institute's analysis, agencies with heavy direct LEP contact tend to keep doing it out of practical necessity), but it's no longer guaranteed the way it was. If federal-level language support becomes less reliable over time, that's a real argument *for* CaseWhy building its own multilingual layer rather than assuming USCIS's own materials will always be there to lean on — a timing/positioning point, not just a translation checklist item.

**Recommendation: don't spread across many languages at once — Spanish, alone, first.** The population gap is too lopsided to treat this as a top-10 rollout. Spanish LEP speakers outnumber the *combined* next nine languages on the list. One well-executed Spanish translation reaches an order of magnitude more people than doing five languages at 20% effort each. Once Spanish is live and there's real usage data, Chinese (Simplified) and Vietnamese are the next clear candidates by population; Haitian Creole is worth watching separately — smaller nationally, but Florida (CaseWhy's own home base) has one of the largest Haitian communities in the country, so real-world signal from local outreach/community validation conversations could reasonably move it up the list ahead of its raw national ranking.

## How — this needs two very different tracks, not one "translate the site" task

**Track 1 — static/marketing content: lower risk, do this first.** The landing page, `/plus`, `/processing-times`, `/visa-bulletin`, `/news`, nav/UI strings, ToS, and privacy policy are all fixed text that doesn't change per-user. Standard approach: Next.js's App Router i18n (the current standard library is `next-intl`, URL-based locale routing like `/es/...`) with professionally translated or AI-drafted-then-native-reviewed copy. This is genuinely low-risk work, and the SEO upside is real and immediate — a Spanish-indexed landing page alone, even before the rest of the product is translated, captures search traffic and community interest CaseWhy currently can't reach at all. Legal pages need one explicit line of real legal language (not guessed at here) establishing that the English version controls in case of any conflict between versions — fold this into the same attorney review already deferred for the English ToS/billing terms, rather than opening a separate legal question.

**Track 2 — the dynamic, per-user AI content: the real product, and the real risk.** `explainCaseStatus()` and the `/ask` chat are CaseWhy's actual core value — "explain what your status means and what to do next." Getting this wrong in translation isn't a typo, it's telling someone the wrong thing about their immigration case. Two real design questions, not just a translation-vendor choice:

- **Generate directly in the target language, rather than machine-translating an English draft afterward.** Claude can produce the explanation in Spanish (or whichever language) from the same prompt/knowledge-base grounding used today — likely both cheaper (one model call instead of generate-then-translate) and more natural than a translated-after-the-fact version. This is an architecture choice, not a vendor choice: it changes `explainCaseStatus()`/`chatAboutCase()` to accept a language parameter, not a translation layer bolted on top.
- **This needs the same guardrail-testing discipline CW-31/CW-32 already went through in English, repeated per language, not assumed to carry over for free.** The knowledge base (CW-31) and the adversarial-question guardrail testing that grounded the English chat were real work, done once. A model that reasons carefully in English isn't automatically guaranteed to handle the same nuance and UPL guardrails equally well in another language without the same kind of real testing — this is a genuine per-language verification cost, worth being honest about rather than treating translation as a one-time, done-forever task.

## When

Not now — the English product hasn't reached paid launch yet, and validating product-market fit in English first, with real usage data guiding which language comes next, beats guessing from demographics alone before there's any live signal. The one piece that could reasonably move earlier without waiting on any of that: a Spanish-language marketing/landing page (Track 1, static content only) is cheap enough and low-risk enough that it could go out ahead of full in-product translation, purely for reach and SEO, the same way the processing-times and visa-bulletin pages already double as content-marketing surfaces. Full Track 2 (translated AI explanations, translated chat) is realistic post-launch, once there's a real user base and a first read on whether Spanish-speaking users are actually showing up in meaningful numbers — which the community-validation conversations already planned in the marketing playbook are a natural place to start listening for.
